    About the Dishes

    Where they come from & why they're good for you
    The Hungry Squirrel
    Field notes · page 2

    This week leans the way a Mediterranean-style diet does — vegetables and fruit up front, lean proteins in support (game, fish, a little poultry), olive oil over butter, and one gloriously indulgent cookout night to keep it human. Here's the story and the upside behind each plate.

    Eating Out Loud · p. 101
    Cherry tomato salad, feta & honey

    Flavor Half the tomatoes slow-roasted to jammy sweetness, half left raw and bright — honey and salty feta bridge the two.

    Roots A modern Israeli mezze idea. The tomato is a New World transplant that only reached the eastern Mediterranean a few centuries ago, yet now defines its summer table.

    Good for you Tomatoes are the headline source of lycopene, an antioxidant that becomes more absorbable when cooked — so the roasted half pulls double duty.

    Trivia Cooking concentrates their natural glutamates — that's the savory "umami" hit.

    Matty Matheson · p. 119
    Chopped baba ghanoush

    Flavor Smoke-charred eggplant, nutty tahini, garlic, lemon.

    Roots A Levantine staple (Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Palestine). The Arabic name translates playfully to something like "pampered father," with old folklore of a daughter making soft food for a toothless dad. Tahini is what separates true baba ghanoush from its cousin mutabbal.

    Good for you Naturally vegan and gluten-free, and generally lighter than hummus; eggplant brings fiber and skin-borne antioxidants.

    In the plan Made big on Day 1 — it keeps about five days and becomes the week's grab-and-go lunch with pita.

    South · p. 86
    Peach & tomato salad

    Flavor Ripe peach and heirloom tomato — both sweet and acidic — under a tangy buttermilk-cream dressing.

    Roots A Southern high-summer plate; peaches and tomatoes crest together here in July, and you're squarely in Virginia peach country.

    Good for you Peaches add vitamin C and potassium; the touch of dairy fat helps you absorb the fruit's fat-soluble antioxidants.

    Trivia Peaches and tomatoes share aroma compounds — part of why they taste so right together.

    Six Seasons · p. 201
    Cucumbers, scallions, mint & chiles

    Flavor Cool and crisp, herbal, with a chile bite and a vinegar snap.

    Roots Cucumber-and-vinegar salads circle the globe; this one leans Southeast-Asian in spirit.

    Good for you Cucumber is about 95% water — hydrating and barely-there on calories; mint eases digestion.

    In the plan Day 3 palate-cleanser against the rich venison, timed while the cukes are still crisp. (Botanically, cucumbers are berries.)

    Six Seasons · p. 210
    Squash ribbons with tomatoes

    Flavor Raw ribboned summer squash, cherry tomatoes, peanuts and herbs in a spicy fish-sauce dressing — fresh, crunchy, savory.

    Roots A Southeast-Asian-inflected treatment; shaving squash raw is a modern vegetable-forward move.

    Good for you Summer squash is light and high-water with vitamin C and B6; the peanuts add protein and crunch.

    Trivia Summer squash is picked immature — skin, seeds and all are edible.

    Six Seasons · p. 116
    Roasted radishes, brown butter & honey

    Flavor Roasting flips radishes from peppery-sharp to sweet and mellow; brown butter, chile and honey finish them.

    Roots Usually eaten raw in the West — cooking them is an old French move that modern cooks revived.

    Good for you A brassica, like cabbage and broccoli: very low-calorie, some vitamin C and fiber — and the leafy tops are edible too.

    Trivia One of the fastest crops going: some varieties run seed-to-harvest in three to four weeks.

    Moosewood Daily Special
    Golden summer soup

    Flavor Mellow and brothy, gently sweet from squash, carrot and corn with a little melting cheese.

    Roots A Moosewood standard — the Ithaca vegetarian institution that shaped American meatless cooking from the 1970s on.

    Good for you Carrots bring beta-carotene (vitamin A); a light pot that the crisped bacon or a can of beans turns into a fuller meal.

    In the plan Day 5, timed to use the corn before its sugars turn starchy — sweet corn is best within a day or two of picking.

    Praise the Lard · p. 212
    Buttermilk-spiced cole slaw

    Flavor Crunchy cabbage and carrot in a tangy, spiced buttermilk dressing, from BBQ legend Mike Mills.

    Roots "Coleslaw" comes from the Dutch koolsla — "cabbage salad"; the buttermilk style is Southern barbecue canon.

    Good for you Cabbage is a cruciferous source of vitamins C and K; buttermilk keeps it tangy and lighter than mayo-heavy slaws.

    In the plan Day 7 cookout side — cabbage is the hardiest thing you bought, so it's held for late. Dressed slaw actually improves after a few hours.

    Moosewood Low-Fat · p. 216
    Vegetable lasagne béchamel

    Flavor Creamy white-sauce layers with leeks, carrots and broccoli — comforting and mild.

    Roots Lasagne is among the oldest pasta shapes; the béchamel-layered northern-Italian style (rather than tomato-and-ricotta) is the classic Emilia-Romagna approach.

    Good for you A lighter Moosewood version; leeks (an allium) add prebiotic fiber, and it's a hearty meat-free anchor.

    In the plan Cooked last (Days 8–10) because its leeks keep longest — and it freezes for future weeks. Béchamel is one of the French "mother sauces."

    Foods of the SW Indian Nations · p. 165
    Venison Apache stew

    Flavor A deep, savory braise of lean venison with chiles, hominy and greens.

    Roots Built on the "Three Sisters" (corn, beans, squash) that anchored Southwestern Indigenous diets for millennia. The hominy is nixtamalized corn — kernels cooked in an alkaline bath of lime or wood ash, a technique thousands of years old.

    Good for you Venison is very lean and iron-rich, with far less fat than beef; braising tenderizes the lean leg. Nixtamalization unlocks the corn's niacin — the reason corn-based diets avoided the deficiency disease pellagra.

    In the plan The protein backbone: a Sunday big-cook that carries several days of leftovers.

    Eating Out Loud · p. 194
    Braised striper (Chraime)

    Flavor Fish braised in a bold, garlicky, spicy tomato-and-pepper sauce, brightened with lemon.

    Roots A North African Jewish dish — especially Libyan, Moroccan and Tunisian — whose name comes from the Arabic for "hot." Traditionally a Friday-night Shabbat starter (also Rosh Hashanah and Passover), often called the Sephardic counterpart to Ashkenazi gefilte fish. The fish is meant to be whatever's freshest — so your striper is right in the spirit.

    Good for you Lean, high-protein white fish; the tomato-pepper sauce piles on vitamin C and lycopene.

    In the plan Day 6 — a light, fast midweek protein that reuses the cherry tomatoes and tahini already on hand.

    Day 7 cookout · spicy links
    Sonoran-style bacon-wrapped dogs

    Flavor A bacon-wrapped link in a soft bolillo roll, piled with beans, tomato, onion, mayo and roasted chile — smoky, rich, messy.

    Roots Born in Hermosillo, Sonora (some trace it to the 1940s, popularized in the late 1980s), then carried north into Tucson's street-cart scene — one Tucson spot's version won a James Beard award.

    Good for you The honest indulgence of the week — which is exactly why the crisp, tangy slaw rides shotgun to balance it.

    Trivia The vendors are called dogueros; the dog itself, a dogo.
