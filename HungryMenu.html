Wednesday

Roots & Greens

Beet "red pasta" & wilted kale

Fresh egg pasta stained deep magenta with roasted beet, alongside kale wilted with golden raisins and a pinch of chile.

Coloring pasta with vegetables is old Italian craft — spinach for green, beet or tomato for red — as much about the beauty of the plate as the taste, and the beet goes in whole, fiber and all, rather than being boiled away. The kale beside it plays the opposite game: bitterness met head-on with sweetness and heat, the same balancing act behind southern Italian greens cooked with raisins and pine nuts.

A bottle An earthy medium red — Pinot Noir or a Loire Cabernet Franc — echoes the beets.

Something sweet Dark chocolate, or a simple olive-oil cake.

Thursday

A Bright Fish Braise

Striper chraime

Striper braised in a bold, garlicky, gently spicy tomato-and-pepper sauce, brightened with lemon.

Chraime is a North African Jewish dish — Libyan, Tunisian, Moroccan — and its name comes straight from the Arabic for "hot." It's traditionally a Friday-night and holiday plate, the Sephardic counterpart to Ashkenazi gefilte fish, and it's forgiving by design: the sauce was built to carry whatever firm fish a household had, so it takes well to almost any kind.

A bottle A chilled light red (Grenache) or a bold, dry rosé to ride the spice.

Something sweet Orange-blossom semolina cake, or a clean citrus sorbet.

Friday

A Sonoran Supper

Sonoran-style bacon-wrapped dogs & buttermilk slaw

Bacon-wrapped links tucked into soft rolls, piled with beans, tomato and onion, with a crisp, tangy slaw.

The Sonoran dog is border food with a real hometown: Hermosillo, the capital of Sonora, where late-night cart vendors — dogueros — wrapped franks in bacon and buried them in toppings before the style crossed north to become a signature of Tucson. The bacon was the move that turned an imported novelty into something unmistakably regional; the full build, con todo, sits far closer to a taco cart's idea of a hot dog than a ballpark's.

A bottle A cold lager, or a juicy, chillable Beaujolais.

Something sweet Lime paletas, or warm cinnamon churros.

The Weekend Table

Cook's-Choice Supper & a Bake

Garden vegetable lasagne

A creamy béchamel lasagne layered with leeks, carrot and broccoli — the easy, comforting close to the week.

This is lasagne in the northern Italian manner — bound with béchamel, one of the French "mother sauces," rather than the tomato-and-ricotta most Americans picture. The shape itself is among the oldest pasta on record, its name traced back through Latin to a Greek word for a flat cooking pot; the ridged sheets are essentially unchanged after centuries.

A rustic country loaf

A crackly-crusted, open-crumb boule baked fresh Saturday morning — bread flour, a little whole wheat, and a long, slow rise.

This is the no-knead method a New York Times column made famous in 2006: time does the work of kneading, and a screaming-hot covered pot stands in for a baker's steam-injected oven, so a home kitchen turns out a bakery-style crust. Mixed the night before and baked in the morning, it carries the weekend — and its stale heel comes back Thursday as the torn croutons for the panzanella, so nothing goes to waste.

A bottle A medium Italian red — Chianti or a bright Barbera.

Something sweet Tiramisu, or a scoop of lemon gelato.

Tuesday

Straight from the Farm

Broiled eggplant with basil

Eggplant blistered under high heat until creamy inside, finished with handfuls of raw basil.

Eggplant made a long journey — out of South Asia, through the Arab world, into the Mediterranean, where cooks eyed it warily for centuries as a suspect member of the nightshade family before it became indispensable. It rewards hard, direct heat: the flesh collapses into something silky and almost custardy, which is why so many traditions char it rather than gently cook it.

Roasted green tomato & feta salad

Green tomatoes roasted with onion until tangy and sweet, with feta, olives and mint.

A green tomato is just an unripe one, and cooking with them began as thrift — a way to rescue fruit that frost or a short season would otherwise waste. Roasting is the gentler path: where frying leans on batter and crunch, the oven softens their sharp acidity into something rounder that can stand up to salty cheese.

A bottle A crisp Mediterranean white or dry rosé — Assyrtiko, Vermentino, or a Provençal rosé.

Something sweet Honey and citrus: a lemon-olive-oil cake or honeyed yogurt with berries.

Wednesday

Golden Hour

Golden summer soup

A mellow, brothy pot of squash, carrot and sweet corn, finished with crisp bacon.

This is a soup that lives and dies by its corn: once picked, sweet corn's sugars start turning to starch within a day or two, so the window for that bright, milky sweetness is short and worth chasing. The recipe comes from the Moosewood kitchen — the Ithaca collective whose cookbooks quietly taught a generation of American home cooks that vegetables could carry a whole meal.

A bottle An unoaked white or an off-dry Riesling — a little sweetness echoes the corn.

Something sweet Sweet-corn ice cream, or a simple buttery pound cake.

Thursday

Green Pasta & a Bread Salad

Kale sauce with farfalle

Kale cooked long and blitzed into a silky emerald sauce caught in the pleats of farfalle, with Parmesan.

It is essentially three ingredients, and it upends what most people expect of kale: cooked well past the point of a quick sauté and puréed, the leaf turns sweet, round and creamy without any cream involved. It follows a very old Italian principle — that a sauce can be one vegetable taken completely seriously — the same logic behind a tomato sauce of nothing but tomatoes.

Roasted pepper panzanella

Blistered sweet peppers and torn bread — the stale heel of the weekend's country loaf — soaked in their own juices, oil and vinegar, with red onion, mint and torn mozzarella.

Panzanella is Tuscan thrift at its best — a whole salad built to rescue stale bread, turning what would be waste into the point of the plate. The trick is timing: the bread wants long enough in the dressing to drink it up and soften, but pulled before it slumps into mush. The peppers are roasted until their skins blister and slip, trading raw snap for something sweet, silky and a little smoky.

A bottle A bright Italian white (Verdicchio, Soave) or a light Barbera.

Something sweet Lemon gelato, or affogato with good espresso.

Friday

The Pantry Pot

French lentil soup

Earthy lentils simmered on a base of bacon, onion, carrot and thyme, with tomato and a bright lift of wine and vinegar at the finish.

Lentils are among the oldest cultivated foods we know of — farmed across the Near East for something like ten thousand years, valued as cheap, storable protein long before anyone had refrigeration. The French treatment leans on the small green Puy-style lentil, which holds its shape instead of dissolving into mush, so the soup stays a dish of distinct beads rather than a purée. The splash of acid at the end does double duty: it wakes up the earthiness, and the vitamin C helps the body take up the iron the lentils carry.

A bottle A rustic French red — Côtes du Rhône, Beaujolais, or a Loire Cabernet Franc.

Something sweet Something homey: an apple tart, or pear poached in red wine.

Saturday

Fresh from the Oven

Zucchini & feta biscuits, with lentil soup

Flaky buttermilk biscuits shot through with grated summer squash, sharp cheddar, feta and dill — warm from the oven beside a bowl of the pantry lentil soup.

This is a happy mash-up: the American buttermilk biscuit, all cold butter and quick hands, folded together with the feta-and-herb instinct of Turkish home baking. Grated squash is the quiet trick — squeezed dry and worked into the dough, it all but vanishes into the crumb, leaving tenderness and a little green fleck behind rather than any obvious vegetable.

A bottle Something easy and unfussy — a dry cider, or a light unoaked white.

Something sweet A homey fruit crumble, or honey-drizzled Turkish-style semolina cake.
